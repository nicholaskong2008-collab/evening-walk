There we were again, on the same road we walk every night. As soon as we were out of the gate, I pulled out my phone to read *The Economist*, like I do on most of our walks. Tonight's piece was about AI leaving rich countries with bigger economies but smaller tax bases. So I asked my dad something like: "Wait, if AI's making companies richer, doesn't Malaysia just get more tax? What's the problem?"

He wasn't convinced, and said it all depends on who actually ends up with the profits.

The article's worry is simple once you see it. AI should make rich economies grow faster, which ought to help governments carrying big debts. But rich-world tax systems mostly tax workers, not capital. If AI moves income away from wages and towards company profits and the people who own shares, a government could end up running a bigger economy with a smaller tax base.

There is already an early sign of this in America. Over the past year, US pre-tax corporate profits rose by about $1.2 trillion, roughly 30%. Corporate tax receipts went the other way, because tech firms are deducting record spending on AI data centres and chips under rules that let them write off investment faster.

So my first reaction was: this is a rich-country problem. Malaysia's tax system looks completely different.

## Malaysia taxes companies, not workers

About three-quarters of US federal tax revenue comes from labour. Malaysia is close to the opposite. In the government's own 2026 budget estimates, companies income tax brings in RM103.4 billion, or 30.1% of all federal revenue. Individual income tax brings in RM49.1 billion, just 14.3%. For every ringgit the government collects from people's pay packets, it collects about two from company profits.

On paper, that should make Malaysia one of the winners in *The Economist*'s story. The magazine found that countries which already tax capital relatively heavily, like Australia and South Korea, come out of an AI-driven shift in income fairly unharmed. If AI pushes Malaysia's national income towards profits, the bigger slice lands in the part of the tax system that already does most of the work.

That was my instinct on the walk, and on the numbers it holds up. But it only holds if the profits are taxed when they arrive.

## The catch: we price the corporate tax near zero for exactly these firms

Malaysia's AI boom so far is mostly a building boom: data centres, especially in Johor. And the incentives used to attract that kind of investment are designed to switch the corporate tax off.

- The standard corporate tax rate is 24%.
- Inside the Johor–Singapore Special Economic Zone, new investment in qualifying activities, including the AI supply chain, can get a 5% rate for up to 15 years.
- Under Malaysia Digital status, a data centre or cloud company can instead claim an investment tax allowance of up to 100% of its qualifying capital spending, set against up to 100% of its taxable income, for up to five years.

That last one is worth pausing on. It is the same mechanism that is draining corporate tax in America: big upfront spending on AI infrastructure written off against profits. The difference is that the US got there partly by accident, through general write-off rules. Malaysia does it on purpose, to win the investment from Singapore.

There is a sensible reason for that. Data centres bring construction work, electricity sales, land deals and some high-skilled jobs. A tax break that lands a project which would otherwise go elsewhere can still leave the country better off. But it means the simple story, "AI raises profits, Malaysia taxes profits, Malaysia wins", has a hole in it. The profits AI creates here are the ones we have chosen to tax least.

## Two things that limit the damage

**First, there is a floor.** Since financial years beginning in 2025, Malaysia has applied the Global Minimum Tax: a 15% minimum effective rate on multinational groups with revenue of at least €750 million. If one of those groups pays less than 15% here, Malaysia charges the difference itself through a Domestic Top-up Tax, so the money is collected here rather than in the parent company's home country. The catch is timing: the first top-up returns and payments aren't due until 30 June 2027, so none of that money shows up yet.

**Second, Malaysia's tax growth is shifting towards consumption.** Sales and service tax is forecast at RM59.6 billion for 2026, after the rate went up and the scope widened. Consumption taxes are one of the fixes *The Economist* says economists are converging on, because they are hard to avoid. Malaysia has been moving that way already, for its own reasons.

## What I'll be watching on Friday

Budget 2027 is tabled on 9 October, and incentives for AI and digital investment are widely expected. Here is my test for whatever gets announced:

1. **Are new AI incentives stacked on old ones, or replacing them?** Every extra allowance shrinks the "we tax profits" advantage.
2. **Are incentives tied to outcomes?** Local jobs, local suppliers and training, rather than capital spending alone, which is the easiest number to inflate.
3. **Does the government count on top-up tax revenue, and when?** If it starts arriving in mid-2027, it should appear in the 2027 revenue estimates.

We both held our ground for the night, and walked back into the house waiting for Friday's budget to settle it.
